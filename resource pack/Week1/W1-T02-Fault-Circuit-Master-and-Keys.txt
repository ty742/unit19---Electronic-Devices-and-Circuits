UNIT 19 – ELECTRONIC DEVICES AND CIRCUITS
LEARNING AIM A

TUTOR RESOURCE

Week: 1
Resource: W1-T02
Title: Fault Circuit Master and Fault Keys

============================================================
PURPOSE
============================================================

This resource provides the tutor with the master circuit,
fault variants and expected troubleshooting routes for the
Week 1 practical challenge:

"CAN YOU MAKE IT WORK?"

The objective is to develop systematic fault-finding rather than
component guessing.

Students should NOT be given the fault key.

============================================================
1. CORE CIRCUIT
============================================================

Recommended circuit:

              +5 V DC
                 |
                 |
                S1
              SWITCH
                 |
                 |
               R1
              330 Ω
                 |
                 |
               D1
               LED
                 |
                 |
                0 V

Functional path:

+5 V → S1 → R1 → D1 → 0 V

Expected behaviour:

S1 OPEN:
LED OFF

S1 CLOSED:
LED ON

Nominal supply:

5 V DC

Nominal resistor:

330 Ω

Typical LED forward voltage:

Approximately 1.8–2.2 V depending on LED type and current.

Approximate current:

I ≈ (5 V - Vf) / 330 Ω

For Vf = 2 V:

I ≈ 9.1 mA

The actual measured value should be used during the practical
rather than assuming an exact value.

============================================================
2. DESIGN PRINCIPLES FOR THE FAULTS
============================================================

Each fault should:

- Be electrically safe.
- Be identifiable using a logical troubleshooting process.
- Require students to inspect and/or measure.
- Have a plausible engineering explanation.
- Avoid requiring specialist equipment.
- Be recoverable within the available lesson time.

Recommended approach:

Use ONE fault per pair initially.

Introduce a second fault only after the learner has successfully
completed the core challenge.

============================================================
3. FAULT A – LED REVERSED
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Reverse the LED polarity.

Expected student symptom:

LED remains OFF when the switch is closed.

Expected student observations:

- Supply should still be present.
- Circuit appears physically complete.
- LED does not illuminate.

Likely student hypothesis:

LED polarity/orientation may be incorrect.

Useful test:

Visual inspection of LED orientation.

Optional measurement:

Measure voltage across LED.

Expected reasoning:

The LED is polarity-sensitive and will not conduct in the intended
direction when reversed.

Repair:

Rotate the LED correctly.

Verification:

Close S1 and confirm the LED illuminates.

Tutor observation focus:

Does the learner identify polarity from the component/schematic
rather than randomly changing components?

============================================================
4. FAULT B – OPEN RESISTOR CONNECTION
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Disconnect one end of R1 from the circuit.

Expected symptom:

LED remains OFF.

Likely hypotheses:

- Open circuit
- Broken connection
- Incorrect breadboard row
- Switch issue

Useful tests:

- Visual inspection
- Continuity check with power OFF
- Voltage measurements

Expected reasoning:

An open connection prevents current flow through the circuit.

Repair:

Reconnect the resistor.

Verification:

Test continuity where appropriate, then safely power the circuit
and confirm operation.

Tutor observation focus:

Does the learner understand why continuity testing should normally
be performed with power OFF?

============================================================
5. FAULT C – INCORRECT BREADBOARD ROW
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Move one jumper/component connection to a non-connected row.

Expected symptom:

Circuit does not operate even though the components themselves
appear correct.

Likely hypotheses:

- Incorrect wiring
- Open connection
- Breadboard misunderstanding

Useful tests:

- Compare physical circuit against schematic.
- Continuity testing where appropriate.
- Trace the circuit path.

Expected reasoning:

The schematic and physical construction do not match electrically.

Repair:

Move the connection to the correct row.

Verification:

Repeat the functional test.

Tutor observation focus:

Can the learner translate the schematic into the breadboard layout?

============================================================
6. FAULT D – SUPPLY CONNECTION MISSING
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Remove the +5 V or 0 V connection.

Expected symptom:

Circuit does not operate.

Useful test:

Measure supply voltage at the relevant circuit test point.

Expected reasoning:

The bench supply may be set correctly, but the circuit is not
receiving the supply because of the missing connection.

Repair:

Restore the correct supply connection.

Verification:

Measure supply at the circuit and perform the functional test.

Tutor observation focus:

Does the learner distinguish between:

"the power supply is working"

and:

"the circuit is receiving the power supply"?

============================================================
7. FAULT E – INCORRECT RESISTOR VALUE
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Replace 330 Ω with a substantially different value.

Recommended teaching version:

Use approximately 1 kΩ for a visible but safe change in LED brightness.

Expected symptom:

LED may illuminate but be noticeably dimmer.

This is intentionally different from a simple "doesn't work"
fault.

Learning opportunity:

The circuit technically functions, but performance does not meet
the expected behaviour.

Useful tests:

- Read resistor value using colour code where appropriate.
- Measure resistance with circuit powered OFF.
- Compare against schematic/component requirement.

Expected reasoning:

The component is present but its value does not match the design.

Repair:

Replace with the specified value.

Verification:

Measure/observe LED operation and confirm the resistor value.

Tutor observation focus:

Can the learner distinguish between:

FAULTY CIRCUIT

and

CIRCUIT THAT WORKS BUT DOES NOT MEET SPECIFICATION?

This distinction is valuable for later analysis/evaluation.

============================================================
8. FAULT F – LOOSE/POOR CONNECTION
============================================================

Fault introduced:

Use a jumper wire or component connection that is not fully
seated.

Expected symptom:

Circuit may operate intermittently.

Challenge:

This is suitable for stronger students.

Student investigation should involve:

- Observation
- Gentle inspection
- Continuity testing with power OFF
- Reproducing the symptom safely
- Identifying the unreliable connection

Important:

Do not encourage students to deliberately manipulate live
connections unsafely.

Repair:

Secure the connection.

Verification:

Repeat the test several times.

Tutor observation focus:

Does the learner recognise that intermittent faults require
repeatability checks?

============================================================
9. FAULT G – TWO FAULTS
============================================================

Recommended only for students who complete the core task early.

Example:

LED reversed
+
incorrect breadboard connection

or:

Open resistor connection
+
incorrect resistor value

Student instruction:

"You have found one problem. The circuit still does not work.
What else could be wrong?"

This reinforces the importance of verification.

============================================================
10. FAULT H – NO SCHEMATIC CHALLENGE
============================================================

Advanced challenge.

Remove the schematic from the student's workstation.

Provide only:

- Physical prototype
- Basic functional requirement
- Component information

Example requirement:

"When the switch is closed, the LED should illuminate from a 5 V
supply."

Student must:

1. Identify components.
2. Infer likely connections.
3. Develop a simple sketch.
4. Identify test points.
5. Diagnose the fault.

Do not use this as the core task for learners who are still
developing basic schematic-to-breadboard skills.

============================================================
11. FAULT ALLOCATION
============================================================

Suggested allocation for a class:

Pair 1:
Fault A – LED reversed

Pair 2:
Fault B – Open resistor connection

Pair 3:
Fault C – Incorrect breadboard row

Pair 4:
Fault D – Missing supply connection

Pair 5:
Fault E – Incorrect resistor value

Pair 6:
Fault F – Loose/intermittent connection

If there are more pairs, repeat the safest faults rather than
introducing increasingly obscure faults.

============================================================
12. EXPECTED TROUBLESHOOTING PATH
============================================================

Students should generally progress through:

1. Identify expected circuit behaviour.

2. Carry out visual inspection.

3. Check supply configuration.

4. Check physical construction against schematic.

5. Make a prediction.

6. Select a measurement/test.

7. Interpret the result.

8. Narrow down the fault.

9. Make the repair.

10. Verify operation.

11. Document the investigation.

Do not require students to follow a rigid test order if they can
justify a different systematic approach.

The key is:

THE TEST MUST HAVE A REASON.

14. EXPECTED TECHNICAL VALUES
============================================================

Nominal supply:

5.0 V DC

Nominal resistor:

330 Ω

Typical LED forward voltage:

Approximately 1.8–2.2 V

Expected current:

Approximately 8–10 mA depending on the LED characteristics.

Example using Vf = 2.0 V:

I = (5 - 2) / 330

I ≈ 9.1 mA

Expected resistor power:

P = I²R

P ≈ (0.0091)² × 330

P ≈ 0.027 W

A standard 0.25 W resistor is therefore comfortably above the
expected dissipation for this simple demonstration circuit.

These are approximate teaching values.

Actual measurements will vary.

15. TUTOR SAFETY CHECK
============================================================

Before the session:

[ ] Faults are electrically safe.

[ ] Supply voltage is appropriate.

[ ] No fault can create excessive current.

[ ] Components are rated appropriately.

[ ] Damaged components are not being used.

[ ] Students cannot access unsafe voltage sources.

[ ] Fault introductions cannot create hazardous conditions.

[ ] Actual laboratory emergency arrangements have been checked.

[ ] Students have been briefed on isolation procedures.

============================================================
16. COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
============================================================

MISCONCEPTION 1:

"If the LED doesn't light, the LED must be broken."

Tutor response:

"What evidence do you have?"

MISCONCEPTION 2:

"The power supply says 5 V, therefore the circuit has 5 V."

Tutor response:

"Where could you measure to prove that?"

MISCONCEPTION 3:

"I changed the component and it worked, so the component was faulty."

Tutor response:

"What test did you perform before replacing it?"

MISCONCEPTION 4:

"The circuit works, so the resistor value doesn't matter."

Tutor response:

"Does the circuit meet its specification?"

MISCONCEPTION 5:

"The fastest team is the best team."

Tutor response:

"Which team produced the strongest evidence?"

============================================================
17. STRETCH QUESTIONS FOR THE TUTOR
============================================================

For strong learners:

1. What if the LED lights but is much too bright?

2. What if the supply voltage is correct but the voltage at the
   circuit is not?

3. How could you distinguish an open circuit from a reversed LED?

4. How could an intermittent fault be confirmed?

5. Why might a wrong resistor value produce a functioning circuit
   rather than a completely failed circuit?

6. What evidence would you include in a professional fault report?

7. What would make you confident enough to sign off the repair?

============================================================
18. PRACTICAL OBSERVATION TARGETS
============================================================

Look particularly for whether learners:

[ ] Stop before powering the circuit.

[ ] Interpret rather than copy the schematic.

[ ] Check the supply.

[ ] Check polarity.

[ ] Make a prediction.

[ ] Select a relevant test.

[ ] Use the multimeter correctly.

[ ] Record measurements.

[ ] Explain what measurements mean.

[ ] Avoid random component replacement.

[ ] Verify the repair.

[ ] Document the result.

============================================================
19. PORTFOLIO EVIDENCE
============================================================

Students should use their selected evidence to complete:

PoE 01 – Prototype Fault Investigation

Strong evidence includes:

- Clear schematic
- Clear circuit photograph
- Relevant measurements
- Logical diagnosis
- Repair record
- Verification result
- Technical reflection

Weak evidence includes:

- "It didn't work."
- "I changed the resistor."
- "It worked afterwards."

Encourage students to explain:

WHAT
→ WHY
→ HOW
→ EVIDENCE
→ RESULT
