Title: Expected Measurements – Prototype Fault-Finding Challenge

============================================================
PURPOSE
============================================================

This resource provides the tutor with expected measurement ranges
for the Week 1 prototype fault-finding circuits.

The values are intentionally approximate.

Students should be encouraged to compare:

PREDICTED
→
MEASURED
→
EXPLAINED

Do not require students to reproduce the exact values below.

Actual values will depend on:

- LED type
- resistor tolerance
- power supply accuracy
- multimeter accuracy
- breadboard connections
- wiring resistance
- temperature
- component characteristics

============================================================
1. CORE CIRCUIT
============================================================

Circuit:

+5 V
  |
 S1
  |
330 Ω
  |
LED
  |
 0 V

Expected function:

S1 OPEN:
LED OFF

S1 CLOSED:
LED ON

Nominal supply:

5.0 V DC

Nominal resistor:

330 Ω

============================================================
2. EXPECTED NORMAL OPERATING VALUES
============================================================

Assume a typical red LED with a forward voltage around 1.8–2.2 V.

Approximate current:

I = (Vs - Vf) / R

For:

Vs = 5 V
Vf = 2.0 V
R = 330 Ω

I ≈ 9.1 mA

Therefore, a practical measurement might reasonably be around:

8–10 mA

depending on the actual LED and resistor.

Expected approximate voltage across the LED:

Approximately 1.7–2.2 V

Expected approximate voltage across the resistor:

Approximately 2.8–3.3 V

The three voltages should approximately satisfy:

Vs ≈ VR + VLED

============================================================
3. MEASUREMENT 1 – SUPPLY VOLTAGE
============================================================

Measurement points:

+5 V rail to 0 V

Expected:

Approximately 5 V DC

Possible normal range for this teaching circuit:

Approximately 4.9–5.1 V

Interpretation:

If the supply is approximately correct:

The bench supply is probably not the primary fault.

If the supply is approximately 0 V:

Investigate:

- Supply switched OFF
- Incorrect supply terminals
- Broken supply connection
- Missing ground/return connection
- Faulty bench supply
- Incorrect circuit connection

Tutor question:

"The PSU says 5 V. Does that prove the circuit is receiving 5 V?"

Expected answer:

No.

The voltage should be measured at the appropriate circuit
test points.

============================================================
4. MEASUREMENT 2 – VOLTAGE ACROSS LED
============================================================

Measurement points:

Across D1.

Expected when correctly forward biased:

Approximately 1.8–2.2 V for a typical red LED.

Expected when LED is OFF due to an open circuit:

The voltage may vary depending on where the open circuit is.

Use the measurement as evidence rather than teaching one fixed
"fault voltage."

IMPORTANT:

Do not give students the expected fault signature in advance.

============================================================
5. MEASUREMENT 3 – VOLTAGE ACROSS RESISTOR
============================================================

Measurement points:

Across R1.

Expected when LED is operating:

Approximately 2.8–3.3 V for a typical red LED.

Relationship:

VR ≈ Vs - VLED

Example:

5.0 V - 2.0 V = 3.0 V

A measured value around 3 V would therefore be reasonable.

============================================================
6. MEASUREMENT 4 – CIRCUIT CURRENT
============================================================

Expected current:

Approximately 8–10 mA

For an example calculation:

I = (5 - 2) / 330

I ≈ 9.1 mA

IMPORTANT:

The tutor must demonstrate correct current-measurement technique.

Students should never connect an ammeter directly across the supply.

============================================================
7. CONTINUITY TESTING
============================================================

Continuity testing should normally be performed with the circuit:

POWER OFF

Possible tests:

- Jumper connection
- Resistor lead path
- Switch contact
- Breadboard connection
- Supply path

Expected result depends on the point being tested.

Teach students that:

"Continuity" does not mean "the circuit is working."

It only provides evidence about whether an electrical path exists
between the selected test points.

============================================================
8. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT A
============================================================

FAULT:

LED reversed.

Expected:

Supply voltage:
Approximately 5 V

Resistor:
Potential voltage/current readings depend on circuit condition.

LED:
Does not illuminate.

Likely useful investigation:

Visual polarity check.

Possible additional test:

Measure the voltage across the LED.

Teaching point:

A polarity-sensitive component can prevent expected operation even
when the component itself is not damaged.

============================================================
9. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT B
============================================================

FAULT:

Open resistor connection.

Expected:

Supply:
Approximately 5 V

LED:
OFF

Circuit current:
Approximately 0 A

A voltage may appear across an open section depending on the exact
location of the break.

Useful investigation:

- Visual inspection
- Continuity test with power OFF
- Voltage measurements

Teaching point:

An open circuit interrupts the current path.

============================================================
10. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT C
============================================================

FAULT:

Incorrect breadboard row.

Expected:

Circuit:
Does not operate normally.

Supply:
May still measure correctly at the supply terminals.

Useful investigation:

Compare:

SCHEMATIC
against
PHYSICAL CONNECTIONS

Then use continuity and voltage checks where appropriate.

Teaching point:

"The power supply is working" does not mean "the circuit is wired
correctly."

============================================================
11. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT D
============================================================

FAULT:

Supply connection missing.

Expected:

Bench PSU may display:

Approximately 5 V

However:

The relevant circuit test point may measure approximately 0 V.

Teaching point:

Always distinguish:

SOURCE VOLTAGE

from

VOLTAGE ACTUALLY PRESENT AT THE CIRCUIT.

============================================================
12. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT E
============================================================

FAULT:

Incorrect resistor value.

Example:

1 kΩ fitted instead of 330 Ω.

Expected:

LED may still illuminate.

Current will be lower.

Approximate current:

I ≈ (5 - 2) / 1000

I ≈ 3 mA

LED will normally appear significantly dimmer.

Teaching point:

A circuit can:

WORK

but

NOT MEET ITS DESIGN REQUIREMENT.

This distinction is valuable for later evaluation work.

============================================================
13. EXPECTED RESULTS – FAULT F
============================================================

FAULT:

Loose/intermittent connection.

Expected:

Circuit may:

- Work sometimes
- Fail when disturbed
- Produce inconsistent measurements

Useful investigation:

- Visual inspection
- Safe inspection of connections
- Continuity testing with power OFF
- Repeat functional testing

Teaching point:

An intermittent fault must be tested for repeatability.

============================================================
14. EXPECTED VALUES – QUICK REFERENCE
============================================================

| Parameter | Approximate expected value |
|-----------|----------------------------|
| Supply voltage | 5.0 V |
| LED forward voltage | 1.8–2.2 V |
| Resistor voltage | 2.8–3.3 V |
| Circuit current | 8–10 mA |
| 330 Ω resistor power | approximately 0.02–0.04 W |

These values are approximate and should not be presented to
students as exact answers.

============================================================
15. TOLERANCE DISCUSSION
============================================================

If R1 is:

330 Ω ±5%

Possible resistance range:

Lower:

330 × 0.95 = 313.5 Ω

Upper:

330 × 1.05 = 346.5 Ω

Therefore, a measured resistance somewhere within this approximate
range could be entirely reasonable.

Use this as a very short stretch discussion if appropriate.

Do not let tolerance discussion overwhelm the Week 1 fault-finding
objective.

============================================================
16. TROUBLESHOOTING QUESTIONS
============================================================

If the supply measures correctly but the LED is OFF:

Ask:

"What does that rule out?"

"What remains possible?"

"What would you test next?"

If current is approximately 0 mA:

Ask:

"Why might no current be flowing?"

If current is much higher than expected:

Ask:

"What could cause excessive current?"

"Is it safe to continue?"

If the LED is dim:

Ask:

"Is the circuit completely faulty?"

"Could the circuit be functioning outside its intended specification?"

============================================================
17. ACCEPTABLE MEASUREMENT DIFFERENCES
============================================================

Do not establish a rigid percentage limit for every measurement
in this first lesson.

Instead introduce the principle:

Measurements are affected by:

- Component tolerance
- Instrument accuracy
- Supply variation
- Connection resistance
- Measurement method

Students should learn to ask:

"Is this result reasonable for the circuit?"

This becomes more formally developed in Week 6.

============================================================
18. TUTOR QUESTIONING AROUND RESULTS
============================================================

Avoid:

"What should the answer be?"

Prefer:

"What did you expect?"

"What did you measure?"

"Is the difference significant?"

"What could explain it?"

"What evidence supports your explanation?"

"What would you measure next?"

"Would you sign off the circuit?"

============================================================
19. COMMON MEASUREMENT MISCONCEPTIONS
============================================================

MISCONCEPTION:

"The meter says 5 V, therefore everything is fine."

RESPONSE:

"Where exactly did you measure it?"

------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION:

"Zero volts means the component is broken."

RESPONSE:

"Where did you measure the zero volts?"

------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION:

"A continuity beep means the circuit works."

RESPONSE:

"What exactly did you prove with the continuity test?"

------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION:

"The reading is different from the textbook value, so it is
wrong."

RESPONSE:

"What factors could make a real measurement different?"

============================================================

[ ] Confirm current measurement method.

[ ] Explain probe placement.

[ ] Reinforce power-OFF requirement for resistance/continuity
    measurements.

[ ] Ensure students understand the risks of incorrect meter
    connections.

[ ] Monitor practical work actively.

============================================================
21. EXPECTED EVIDENCE FOR POE 01
============================================================

Students do not need to submit every measurement.

Strong evidence should include measurements that help demonstrate:

1. What the circuit should do.

2. What was actually happening.

3. How the fault was located.

4. How the repair was verified.

Encourage students to select evidence rather than simply
collecting large quantities of data.
