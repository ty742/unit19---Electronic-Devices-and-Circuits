============================================================
1. SESSION INFORMATION
============================================================

Duration:
3 hours including a 20-minute break

Active teaching and learning time:
160 minutes

Learning Aim:
A – Explore the safe operation and applications of analogue devices and circuits that form the building blocks of commercial circuits.

Pearson content focus:
A1 – Safe electronic working practices
Introduction to A5 – Schematic capture and simulation
Introduction to A6 – Testing physical analogue circuits

Workplace role:
Junior Electronics Technician

Workplace scenario:
A prototype electronic control board has failed its bench test.
The engineering team has been asked to inspect, diagnose, repair and
verify the circuit.

Core challenge:
"CAN YOU MAKE IT WORK?"

Core teaching strategy:
Problem-based learning
Exploratory learning
Learning by doing
Paired learning
Build → Test → Break → Fix → Repeat
Predict → Test → Explain

Portfolio of Evidence:
PoE 01 – Prototype Fault Investigation

Assignment relationship:
Introduce the overall summative assignment and show learners that
their practical work across the seven weeks will progressively generate
evidence that can be developed into the final submission.

============================================================
2. LEARNING OBJECTIVES
============================================================

By the end of the lesson, learners should be able to:

KNOW

1. Explain why safe working practices are essential when constructing
   and testing electronic circuits.

2. Identify common electronic hazards associated with low-voltage
   electronic laboratory work.

3. Explain the purpose of isolation, emergency procedures and
   reporting routes.

4. Recognise common electronic components and their schematic symbols.

5. Explain the basic purpose of a breadboard and multimeter.

DEMONSTRATE

6. Identify components from their physical appearance and/or markings.

7. Interpret a simple electronic schematic.

8. Carry out a pre-power safety inspection.

9. Construct/check a simple low-voltage circuit on a breadboard.

10. Safely use a bench power supply and multimeter for basic checks.

11. Apply a systematic troubleshooting process.

12. Diagnose and rectify a simple circuit fault.

13. Provide basic technical evidence of the work completed.

============================================================
3. SUCCESS CRITERIA
============================================================

By the end of the session, a learner working securely should be able to:

[ ] Identify the main components in the challenge circuit.

[ ] Identify the main hazards before powering the circuit.

[ ] Complete a pre-power check without prompting.

[ ] Explain what the circuit is supposed to do.

[ ] Build/check the circuit correctly.

[ ] Use a multimeter appropriately.

[ ] Gather evidence before changing components.

[ ] Identify the fault using evidence.

[ ] Verify that the repair has worked.

[ ] Record the investigation in the Portfolio of Evidence.

5. PRIOR PREPARATION
============================================================

Before students arrive:

1. Place one faulty circuit at each practical station.

2. Prepare different faults for different groups so copying is reduced.

3. Keep one fully working version at the tutor station.

4. Label each circuit with a circuit ID.

5. Prepare printed schematics.

6. Set out component trays.

7. Check bench supplies are initially OFF.

8. Prepare multimeters.

9. Open the digital simulation environment.

10. Display the title:

   "CAN YOU MAKE IT WORK?"

11. Display the workplace scenario:

   "A prototype board has failed its bench test.
    Your team has been asked to find out why."

12. Have Portfolio of Evidence templates available.

6. FAULT CIRCUIT DESIGN
============================================================

Recommended basic circuit:

5 V supply
    |
  Switch
    |
 Resistor
    |
   LED
    |
   0 V

Each faulty version should contain ONE primary fault.

Possible faults:

Fault A:
LED installed backwards.

Fault B:
Open resistor connection.

Fault C:
Incorrect breadboard row.

Fault D:
Missing supply connection.

Fault E:
Incorrect resistor value.

Fault F:
Loose/poor jumper connection.

Optional second-stage faults:

Fault G:
Two faults introduced.

Fault H:
Intermittent connection.

IMPORTANT:

The fault should be discoverable through a logical troubleshooting
process. Avoid faults that can only be solved by guessing.

============================================================
7. SESSION TIMELINE
============================================================

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 1 – HOOK AND DIAGNOSTIC
TIME: 0–15 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Tutor action:

Display the failed prototype scenario.

Ask:

"You have been given a circuit that doesn't work.
What is the FIRST thing you do?"

Students individually write three actions.

Pair discussion follows.

Tutor captures responses on the board.

Likely answers:

- Look at the circuit
- Check power
- Check wiring
- Measure voltage
- Check components
- Ask someone
- Replace the component

Do not immediately tell learners which are correct.

Introduce the idea:

"Good engineers do not start by randomly changing components."

Introduce the basic process:

OBSERVE
→ PREDICT
→ TEST
→ LOCATE
→ FIX
→ VERIFY

Purpose:

Diagnose existing knowledge and establish the engineering mindset.

Key questioning:

- Why shouldn't we immediately replace the LED?
- What information do we already have?
- What could make the test unsafe?
- What would you check before powering the circuit?

Assessment:

Formative questioning.

Observe learner responses for:

- Prior electronics experience
- Confidence
- Understanding of safety
- Troubleshooting mindset

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 2 – SAFETY AND EMERGENCY SCENARIO
TIME: 15–35 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Workplace scenario:

"You are working on the prototype when you notice smoke coming
from a circuit on the bench."

Groups receive action cards.

Examples:

- Continue troubleshooting
- Isolate/disconnect the supply
- Alert the responsible person
- Raise the alarm where required
- Touch the circuit to see whether it is hot
- Leave the laboratory when instructed
- Record/report the incident
- Restart the power

Task:

Arrange the actions into a safe sequence.

Tutor then demonstrates the actual laboratory arrangements:

- Electrical isolation
- Emergency stop
- Relevant heat-source isolation if applicable
- Fire alarm
- Evacuation route
- Assembly point
- First-aid arrangements
- Reporting procedures

Emphasise:

DO NOT:

- Touch an overheating circuit
- Continue testing after an unsafe condition
- Randomly disconnect live connections
- Attempt unauthorised repairs

Question:

"What is the difference between noticing a hazard and controlling
the hazard?"

Portfolio connection:

Students will later include the safety/pre-power checklist
in PoE 01.

Assessment:

Question individual learners about:

- How to isolate
- Who to inform
- What to do during an emergency
- Why instructions must be followed

SEND support:

Use visual safety icons and a simple sequence:

STOP
→ ISOLATE
→ ALERT
→ FOLLOW INSTRUCTIONS
→ REPORT

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 3 – SCHEMATIC TO PHYSICAL CIRCUIT
TIME: 35–55 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Display the challenge circuit schematic.

Students identify:

- Supply
- 0 V
- Switch
- Resistor
- LED
- Current path

Introduce:

"A schematic is a communication tool used by engineers."

Tutor demonstrates:

Schematic symbol
→ Physical component
→ Breadboard position

Quick activity:

"POINT TO IT"

Tutor points to a schematic symbol.

Learners identify the physical component.

Reverse challenge:

Tutor shows a physical component.

Learners identify the symbol.

Introduce basic breadboard concepts:

- Power rails
- Connected rows
- Centre gap
- Component placement
- LED polarity
- Jumper wires

DO NOT provide a long breadboard lecture.

The goal is only to give learners enough information to complete
the practical.

Assessment:

Circulate and ask:

"Show me where this component is represented on the schematic."

SEND support:

- Enlarged schematic
- Colour-coded wires
- Component photographs
- Breadboard map
- Numbered connection steps

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 4 – BUILD/CHECK WITHOUT POWER
TIME: 55–75 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Student task:

"Build/check the circuit, but DO NOT connect the power supply."

Students:

1. Identify components.
2. Follow the schematic.
3. Check polarity.
4. Check resistor value.
5. Check jumper connections.
6. Check for unintended shorts.
7. Complete the pre-power checklist.

Tutor introduces:

"Pre-power inspection."

Students request tutor inspection before power is connected.

Important:

Do not reward students simply because their LED eventually lights.

The safety process is part of the learning outcome.

Tutor questioning:

- What could happen if the LED is reversed?
- What happens if the supply is incorrect?
- Why check the resistor value?
- How would you detect an unintended short?
- Why should the power remain OFF during this check?

Assessment:

Tutor observation.

Record:

SAFE
PARTIALLY SECURE
NEEDS SUPPORT

------------------------------------------------------------
BREAK
TIME: 75–95 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

20 minutes.

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 5 – POWER, MEASURE AND TROUBLESHOOT
TIME: 95–130 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Students begin the actual fault-finding challenge.

Sequence:

1. Confirm supply setting.
2. Connect supply.
3. Observe the circuit.
4. Record the symptom.
5. Predict possible causes.
6. Select an appropriate test.
7. Measure.
8. Locate the fault.
9. Correct the fault.
10. Retest.
11. Verify operation.

Required troubleshooting method:

OBSERVE
→ PREDICT
→ TEST
→ LOCATE
→ FIX
→ VERIFY

Tutor should avoid giving the answer.

Use questioning instead:

"What makes you think that?"

"What measurement could prove it?"

"What result would you expect if that were the fault?"

"If that measurement is correct, what can we rule out?"

"Can you prove the repair worked?"

If a learner begins random component replacement:

Stop and ask:

"Show me the evidence for changing that component."

This reinforces engineering discipline.

Assessment:

Tutor observation of:

- Safe power-up
- Meter use
- Logical troubleshooting
- Evidence-based decision making
- Correct repair
- Verification

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 6 – SECOND FAULT / CHALLENGE
TIME: 130–145 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Students who finish early receive a second challenge.

Options:

1. A second deliberate fault.
2. A fault on another team's circuit.
3. A circuit without the schematic.
4. A circuit with an ambiguous symptom.

Challenge:

"Can you diagnose the fault without guessing?"

For strong learners:

Ask them to write a short troubleshooting procedure that
another engineer could follow.

For learners requiring support:

Use the troubleshooting flowchart.

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 7 – PORTFOLIO OF EVIDENCE
TIME: 145–155 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Students create:

PoE 01 – Prototype Fault Investigation

Required evidence:

1. Safety/pre-power checklist
2. Annotated schematic
3. Photograph of physical circuit
4. Measurements
5. Fault diagnosis
6. Corrective action
7. Verification result
8. Short reflection

Reflection prompts:

"I originally thought..."

"I tested..."

"I discovered..."

"Next time I would..."

Tutor checks that students have sufficient evidence before leaving.

------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 8 – EXIT TICKET
TIME: 155–160 MINUTES
------------------------------------------------------------

Students answer:

1. What must you do before powering an electronic circuit?

2. Why should an engineer avoid random component replacement?

3. What makes a fault diagnosis different from a guess?

Optional challenge:

"You measure the correct supply voltage but the LED still doesn't
light. Give two sensible next checks."

Collect or digitally submit the exit ticket.

- Troubleshooting flowchart
- Measurement prompts
- Worked example
- Tutor checkpoint system

Suggested practical roles:

BUILDER
TESTER
RECORDER

Rotate roles if time permits.

SEND SUPPORT

- Large clear diagrams
- Minimal text per instruction
- One action at a time
- Component photographs
- Symbol + photograph together
- Colour coding
- Vocabulary bank
- Sentence starters
- Demonstrate before independent work
- Oral explanation before written reflection
- Paired learning

Avoid unnecessary reduction of technical content.
Reduce the language/organisation burden while maintaining the

============================================================
9. STRETCH AND CHALLENGE
============================================================

LEVEL 1:

Find one known fault.

LEVEL 2:

Find two faults.

LEVEL 3:

Diagnose the fault without being told the likely fault location.

LEVEL 4:

Explain which measurement proves the fault.

LEVEL 5:

Write a troubleshooting process another technician could use.

LEVEL 6:

Act as the "senior technician" and verify another team's diagnosis.

Strong learners should receive more decision-making, not simply
more written questions.

============================================================
10. TUTOR QUESTIONING
============================================================

Use questions such as:

"What's the circuit supposed to do?"

"What evidence do you have?"

"What would you expect to measure?"

"What measurement would distinguish between those two possibilities?"

"Why did you choose that test?"

"What have you ruled out?"

"What is your next hypothesis?"

"How do you know the repair has worked?"

"What would you record for another engineer?"

"Would you be happy to sign off this repair?"

Avoid questions that only require recall where possible.

============================================================
13. ASSIGNMENT INTRODUCTION
============================================================

Introduce the summative assignment at an overview level.

Students should understand:

1. The overall engineering scenario.
2. That diode, transistor and op-amp investigations will form the
   core technical work.
3. That simulation and physical testing will be required.
4. That results will need to be explained, analysed and evaluated.
5. That evidence will be generated progressively.

Avoid asking learners to write the full assignment today.

Message to students:

"You are going to build this assignment by doing real engineering
work over the next seven weeks."

============================================================
15. EQUALITY, DIVERSITY AND INCLUSION
============================================================

Ensure all learners have meaningful roles in practical work.

Rotate roles so that the same students do not always:

- Build
- Measure
- Record
- Lead

Use mixed-ability collaboration where appropriate.

Value different approaches to troubleshooting while requiring
all conclusions to be evidence-based.

Ensure challenge is accessible through reasoning rather than
physical speed or prior electronics experience alone.

============================================================
16. SAFEGUARDING / PREVENT / COLLEGE VALUES
============================================================

Embed through professional behaviour rather than a separate lecture.

Reinforce:

- Following legitimate safety instructions
- Reporting concerns through the correct route
- Respectful communication
- Responsible use of laboratory equipment
- Respect for shared resources
- Accountability for one's own actions
- Not bypassing safety controls
- Speaking up when something is unsafe

============================================================
17. LESSON REVIEW / TUTOR NOTES
============================================================

After the session, record:

1. Were students able to perform pre-power checks independently?

2. Which components were most difficult to identify?

3. Which multimeter functions caused difficulty?

4. Did students use evidence rather than guessing?

5. Which SEND strategies were most effective?

6. Which students need additional support before Week 2?

7. Were there any safety misconceptions?

8. Did the faulty circuits provide an appropriate level of challenge?

9. Did students successfully create PoE 01?

10. What should be revisited briefly at the start of Week 2?

============================================================
18. EXPECTED END-OF-SESSION POSITION
============================================================

Every learner should leave with:

- A basic understanding of safe electronic working.
- Experience interpreting a simple schematic.
- Experience checking/constructing a breadboard circuit.
- Experience making basic measurements.
- Experience troubleshooting systematically.
- A completed or substantially completed PoE 01.
- An understanding that the final assignment will be built
  progressively through practical engineering work.

The key message students should leave with is:

"Don't guess. Don't rush. Make it safe, gather evidence, test your
idea, fix the problem and prove that the repair worked."
