LEARNING AIM A

Title: Tutor Questioning Guide – Prototype Fault-Finding Challenge

============================================================
PURPOSE
============================================================

This guide supports tutor questioning during Week 1.

The purpose is to move learners from:

"I think..."

towards:

"I think...because...and I can test this by..."

Questions should encourage:

- Recall
- Observation
- Prediction
- Evidence-based reasoning
- Safe working
- Technical communication
- Problem solving
- Reflection

Avoid giving the fault away through questioning.

The objective is to help learners THINK like technicians.

============================================================
1. QUESTIONING PRINCIPLES
============================================================

Prefer:

"What do you observe?"

over:

"What's wrong?"

Prefer:

"What do you expect to measure?"

over:

"What should the answer be?"

Prefer:

"What evidence supports that?"

over:

"Are you sure?"

Prefer:

"What could you test to distinguish between those possibilities?"

over:

"Check the resistor."

Prefer:

"How do you know the repair worked?"

over:

"Is it fixed?"

The central question throughout the practical is:

"WHAT EVIDENCE DO YOU HAVE?"

============================================================
2. ENGINEERING TOOLBOX – OPENING RECALL
============================================================

Use these during the first 5–10 minutes.

### Component recall

1. Identify this component.

2. What is its schematic symbol?

3. What does it do?

4. Does it have a polarity/orientation?

### Circuit recall

5. What is the purpose of the resistor?

6. What should happen when the switch is open?

7. What should happen when the switch is closed?

### Measurement recall

8. What could you measure with a multimeter?

9. Which measurement would you use to check whether a circuit
   node is receiving the supply voltage?

10. Which measurement would normally be performed with power OFF?

### Safety recall

11. What should happen before the circuit is powered?

12. What should you do if you are unsure whether the circuit is safe?

13. Where is the electrical isolation point in this laboratory?

### Troubleshooting recall

14. What is our troubleshooting sequence?

Expected:

OBSERVE → PREDICT → TEST → LOCATE → FIX → VERIFY

Do not insist on exact wording if the learner demonstrates the
correct process.

============================================================
3. SCHEMATIC INTERPRETATION QUESTIONS
============================================================

Ask before students touch the physical circuit.

1. "What is the purpose of this circuit?"

2. "Where does the power enter?"

3. "Where does the current return?"

4. "Can you trace the complete electrical path?"

5. "Which component controls the current?"

6. "Which component is polarity-sensitive?"

7. "What should happen when the switch is closed?"

8. "What would happen if the LED were reversed?"

9. "Where would you expect to find the supply voltage?"

10. "What measurement points would be useful?"

### Deeper questions

"What would another engineer need to know before building this?"

"Does the physical circuit appear to match the schematic?"

"What would make you stop before powering it?"

============================================================
4. PRE-POWER QUESTIONING
============================================================

Before allowing power to be connected:

1. "What have you checked?"

2. "Why did you check it?"

3. "What could happen if that check was skipped?"

4. "What supply voltage does the circuit require?"

5. "What is the supply actually set to?"

6. "How do you know?"

7. "Have you checked polarity?"

8. "Which component requires particular attention?"

9. "What do you expect to happen when power is applied?"

10. "What will you measure first?"

### Challenge

"Suppose the circuit is safe to power, but you don't know what
result to expect. Should you power it?"

Encourage:

"Not until I understand the expected behaviour."

============================================================
5. TROUBLESHOOTING QUESTIONS
============================================================

## STEP 1 – OBSERVE

Ask:

"What should happen?"

"What actually happened?"

"What is the difference?"

"Can you describe the symptom without saying what you think
caused it?"

This last question is useful because it separates observation
from assumption.

------------------------------------------------------------
## STEP 2 – PREDICT

Ask:

"What could cause that symptom?"

"Can you give me three possibilities?"

"Which is most likely?"

"Why?"

"What evidence do you already have?"

Avoid accepting:

"The LED is broken."

Ask:

"What makes you think that?"

------------------------------------------------------------
## STEP 3 – TEST

Ask:

"What could you measure?"

"Where would you measure?"

"Which instrument would you use?"

"What mode should the meter be in?"

"What do you expect to see?"

"Why is that test useful?"

"Would the test be safe with the circuit powered?"

------------------------------------------------------------
## STEP 4 – LOCATE

After the learner measures:

"What did you expect?"

"What did you get?"

"What does that tell you?"

"What does it rule out?"

"What remains possible?"

"What would you test next?"

------------------------------------------------------------
## STEP 5 – FIX

Before the learner changes anything:

"What exactly are you going to change?"

"Why?"

"What evidence supports that decision?"

"What do you predict will happen?"

This prevents trial-and-error replacement.

------------------------------------------------------------
## STEP 6 – VERIFY

After repair:

"How will you know that the repair has worked?"

"What measurement should you repeat?"

"Does the result match the expected result?"

"Would you be comfortable signing off the circuit?"

"Could the circuit be working but still not meet its requirement?"

This last question introduces the idea of specification and
evaluation early.

============================================================
6. QUESTIONS FOR DIFFERENT LEARNER NEEDS
============================================================

### Learner is stuck

Use a scaffold:

1. "What should happen?"
2. "What actually happened?"
3. "What is different?"
4. "What could cause that difference?"
5. "What can you measure?"
6. "What do you expect?"
7. "What did you find?"

Do not immediately give the answer.

------------------------------------------------------------
### Learner is guessing

Ask:

"What evidence do you have?"

"What test would prove that?"

"Could something else cause the same symptom?"

------------------------------------------------------------
### Learner is overconfident

Ask:

"How can you verify that?"

"What result would prove you wrong?"

"Would another engineer accept that as sufficient evidence?"

------------------------------------------------------------
### Learner lacks technical vocabulary

Offer:

"Try completing this sentence:

'I think the fault may be ______ because ______.'"

or:

"The measurement shows ______, which suggests ______."

============================================================
7. QUESTIONS FOR SEND SUPPORT
============================================================

Use short, concrete questions.

Instead of:

"Can you analyse what's happening?"

Try:

"What should happen?"

"What happened?"

"What is different?"

"What can you check?"

"What did you measure?"

"What does that tell you?"

"Is it fixed?"

"How do you know?"

Give additional processing time.

Allow learners to answer verbally before writing.

Use the physical circuit while questioning.

Point to the relevant component or test point.

============================================================
8. STRETCH QUESTIONING
============================================================

For stronger learners:

1. "Can you identify two possible faults that would produce the
   same symptom?"

2. "Which test would most efficiently distinguish between them?"

3. "Why is that test better than your first idea?"

4. "What measurement would you expect if your hypothesis were
   wrong?"

5. "Can you design a troubleshooting procedure another technician
   could follow?"

6. "What evidence would be sufficient to sign off the repair?"

7. "What if the circuit works but does not meet specification?"

8. "How would you investigate an intermittent fault?"

9. "How could you make your test more reliable?"

10. "What would you document for the next engineer?"

============================================================
9. TEAMWORK QUESTIONING
============================================================

When students work in pairs, ask:

"Who is responsible for the measurement?"

"Who is recording?"

"Can your partner explain your hypothesis?"

"Can you explain their reasoning?"

"Have you agreed what you are testing?"

"If your partner disagrees, what evidence can settle the
difference?"

This encourages technical discussion rather than passive
group working.

============================================================
10. WORKPLACE-STYLE QUESTIONS
============================================================

Use these to maintain the real-life context.

"You are about to hand this circuit to another engineer.
What information would they need?"

"Would you sign this circuit off?"

"How would you explain the fault to your supervisor?"

"What evidence would you include in a test report?"

"What would you do if the customer says the circuit works
intermittently?"

"What would happen if you replaced the component without
identifying the root cause?"

"How could poor documentation create a problem for the next
engineer?"

============================================================
11. ENGINEERING TOOLBOX – END-OF-LESSON RETRIEVAL
============================================================

Ask verbally or use the exit ticket.

1. What is the first step in troubleshooting?

2. Why do we predict before measuring?

3. What should be checked before power is applied?

4. Why is random component replacement poor engineering practice?

5. How do you verify a repair?

### Optional challenge

"Why might a circuit work but still be considered unacceptable?"

============================================================
12. QUESTIONS LINKED TO PORTFOLIO OF EVIDENCE
============================================================

When reviewing PoE 01:

"What problem were you investigating?"

"What evidence did you collect?"

"Which measurement was most useful?"

"How did you decide where the fault was?"

"Why is your photograph useful?"

"Can another engineer understand your schematic?"

"Does your evidence prove that the repair worked?"

"What would make your evidence stronger?"

============================================================
13. QUESTIONS FOR ASSIGNMENT AWARENESS
============================================================

Keep the assignment discussion high-level in Week 1.

Ask:

"What type of evidence might an engineer need to prove a circuit
works?"

"What might another engineer need to see?"

"Why would simulation evidence be useful?"

"Why might physical-test evidence also be necessary?"

"Why wouldn't a photograph of a working circuit be enough by
itself?"

Use these questions to introduce the idea that the final
assignment will be built from engineering evidence.

============================================================
14. QUESTIONING TO AVOID
============================================================

Avoid questions that reveal the answer.

For example:

DO NOT:

"Have you checked whether the LED is backwards?"

Instead:

"What could cause the LED not to illuminate?"

Then:

"What could you check to investigate that?"

Avoid:

"Measure across the resistor."

Instead:

"Which measurement would help you determine whether current is
flowing?"

Avoid:

"The resistor is in the wrong row, isn't it?"

Instead:

"How could you check whether the physical circuit matches the
schematic?"

17. CORE QUESTION TO REPEAT THROUGHOUT UNIT 19
============================================================

Ask frequently:

"HOW DO YOU KNOW?"

This should become part of the classroom culture.

Student:

"The circuit works."

Tutor:

"How do you know?"

Student:

"The simulation says..."

Tutor:

"How do you know the physical circuit matches the simulation?"

Student:

"The measurement is..."

Tutor:

"How does that measurement support your conclusion?"

This gradually develops evidence-based engineering reasoning.

============================================================
FINAL TUTOR REMINDER
============================================================

The purpose of questioning is not to make the learner eventually
say the answer the tutor already knows.

The purpose is to help the learner develop the habit of:

OBSERVE
→ THINK
→ PREDICT
→ TEST
→ INTERPRET
→ JUSTIFY

That habit will become increasingly important as students move
from practical troubleshooting towards engineering analysis and
evaluation in Weeks 5–7.
